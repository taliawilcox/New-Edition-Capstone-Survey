Introduction

My name is Talia Wilcox, and I am a final year master's candidate at The Fletcher School at Tufts University. I am currently pursuing research on theater nuclear forces and escalation perceptions for my master's capstone project. In order to understand how policymakers and decision-makers perceive theater forces, I have designed a brief scenario and survey to gather data on what the U.S. thinks is usable when crossing the nuclear threshold.

Kaliningrad Crisis Scenario

Background and Assumptions

Russia is concerned about the security of Kaliningrad. To offset the exclave's vulnerability, Russia has deployed both offensive and defensive assets to Kaliningrad. Notable offensive capabilities include long-range strike systems like the Iskander missile and hypersonic weapons like the Kinzhal missile. Kaliningrad also hosts anti-ship missiles and strong air defense systems. Western intelligence suggests Kaliningrad also hosts a buried nuclear weapons storage facility.

In response, Lithuania, NATO's leader in defense spending at 5% of GDP, has heavily fortified its border and hosts the alliance's growing forward deployments. EU sanctions further complicate Russia's access to Kaliningrad. Sanctions prevent transit of specific goods, and security and border closures restrict shipments, leaving Kaliningrad dependent on its port access to make up for shortages. Russian officials increasingly portray NATO's fortification of the Gap and its expanding forward presence as preparation to blockade – or seize – Kaliningrad outright.

Russia's continuous nuclear signaling and recent nuclear sharing arrangement with Belarus—framed as a defensive answer to NATO encirclement—further complicate NATO's calculus, leaving Lithuania surrounded on multiple sides. Russia's November 2024 doctrine update lowered its nuclear threshold. Because Kaliningrad is sovereign Russian territory, Russia regards any blockade of or attack on the exclave as a threat to Russian territorial integrity. Russia also remains unconstrained by arms control treaties or nuclear norms. Putin revoked ratification of the CTBT in 2023, Russian officials declared the Novaya Zemlya test site "fully ready" in 2024, and in 2026 the New START treaty expired with no successor. The US, meanwhile, remains fully committed to NATO's Article 5 and extended deterrence, with nuclear weapons still forward-deployed in Europe under NATO nuclear sharing—deployments Russia has long cited as evidence of NATO's hostile intent.

Response Options
